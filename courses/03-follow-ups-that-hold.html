Follow-ups that do not fall through

Triage decides what you look at. It does not remember what you promised. The follow-up that slips is rarely a message you never saw; it is a sentence you wrote yourself, "I will send it Friday", sitting in your sent folder where nothing will ever surface it again. Tonight we treat the promise as the unit, build a two-column list of what you owe and what you are waiting for, look honestly at what the research does and does not say about why that list helps, and pull the commitments out of ten of your own sent messages.

Part 1 · covers requests and commitments

The unit of a follow-up is a promise

5 min live

A follow-up is not a message. It is one sentence inside a message: a request ("could you send the numbers by Thursday?") or a commitment ("I will send the draft Friday"). Finding those sentences sounds trivial until you ask three careful people to do it on the same pile of email and watch them disagree.

LiveRequests and commitments, and why careful humans disagree3 min▶

A request asks someone to do something: "Could you send the Q3 numbers by Thursday?" A commitment promises that you will: "I will send the draft contract Friday." Every follow-up you will ever chase is one of those two sentences, written by you or to you.

Lampert, Dale and Paris (NAACL 2010) drew 664 messages at random from the Enron corpus and had three annotators mark each one for whether it contained a request for action. Overall agreement was kappa 0.681: well above chance, and well short of perfect. Kappa corrects raw agreement for the agreement you would get by guessing, so 0.681 means trained readers, working from a written definition, still split on a real share of messages. The authors then kept only the 505 messages all three agreed on, and 52.08 percent of those contained a request.

Two lessons follow. First, "contains a request" is not "you will act on it". About half of the agreed messages held a request, and that still does not make every ask a loop you own; plenty are aimed at a list, a team, or someone else in the thread. Second, if careful humans disagree on the edges, any extraction, by you or by a model, needs a review pass. The disagreement is not a defect of the tool; it is a property of the sentences.

The study's own error analysis says where the hard cases are: about 20 percent of the classifier's errors were implicit requests ("it would be good to have the figures before the board meets"), and another 10 percent came from marketing or spam with request-like directives ("Click here to renew").

Self-studyWhy zoning matters: the quoted reply problem3 min▶

An email is not one block of text. It has zones: a greeting, the new text the sender actually wrote, a quoted earlier reply (often several, nested), a signature, sometimes a legal disclaimer. Lampert and colleagues split messages into zones first and only then looked for requests. Message-level accuracy went from 72.28 percent without zoning to 83.76 percent with it, a relative increase of 15.9 percent and a 41 percent reduction in errors.

The reason is in the figure above. A long thread carries every earlier request in its quoted tail. "Can you book the room for the 12th?" was Lee asking someone, possibly you, on Monday, and it may already be done. An extractor that reads the whole message will happily list it again as a fresh request. When you ask an assistant to pull follow-ups out of mail, tell it to read only the new text, and when you check its output, the first thing to look for is a line whose source sentence starts with ">" or sits under "On Mon, Lee wrote:".

Real world

The Enron annotators. The 664 messages Lampert, Dale and Paris used are ordinary business mail, drawn at random from a real company's corpus. Three annotators read every one against the same written definition and still disagreed often enough to land at kappa 0.681. If trained readers working slowly do not agree on what counts as a request, a model skimming your mailbox will not either, and neither will you at 18:40 on a Friday. Build the check into the process instead of hoping the extraction is right.

Part 2 · covers the open-loops list

Two columns: what I owe, what I am waiting for

5 min live

The list has two columns and three feeds. Your sent mail feeds both columns, because you write your own promises and your own asks. Your inbox feeds only one, and only after a decision: a request to you is mail until you accept it, and a promise once you do.

LiveWhy the sent folder is the best source you are not using2 min▶

Most follow-up tools start from the inbox, because that is where the unread count lives. But the promises you are judged on are the ones you made, and those are in your sent mail: "I will send it Friday", "let me check with finance and come back to you", "will do". The inbox is full of requests that may or may not be yours; the sent folder is a list of things you actually said yes to, and a list of things you actually asked for.

Each line on the list keeps three things: the exact sentence that created it (so you can check it later without reopening the thread), the person on the other side, and a date: the date you promised, or for a waiting-for line, the date you will chase. A line without its sentence is a guess about what you said; a line without a date never comes up.

This is not a new idea. The GTD Weekly Review checklist (David Allen Company) has a line for exactly the second column: "Review Waiting For List: Record appropriate actions for any needed follow-up. Check off received ones." That is a practitioner method, written down from practice, not a study; it tells you what experienced people do, not what was measured to work.

Self-studyExtraction is a recall job, so over-extract and you prune3 min▶

Session 2 gave you precision and recall. Use them here. A missed commitment costs you a broken promise, found out by the other person. A spurious candidate costs you two seconds to strike out. Those costs are nowhere near equal, so tune the assistant for recall: ask it to list anything that might be a promise or an ask, including the implied ones, and do the precision pass yourself.

The prompt below carries an honesty clause. The assistant must quote the sentence, not paraphrase it, because a paraphrase can quietly turn "I will try to send it Friday" into "send it Friday". It must mark implied commitments "implicit?" rather than present them as firm, because those are the 20 percent that tripped up the Lampert classifier. And it must say "none found" rather than invent one, because an assistant asked for a list will otherwise produce a list.

★ The extraction prompt, with the honesty clauseBelow are messages I sent. For each one, read ONLY the new text I wrote. Ignore quoted earlier replies (lines starting with ">" or under "On ... wrote:"), signatures and disclaimers. List every sentence that is either: - a COMMITMENT: something I said I would do, or - a REQUEST: something I asked the recipient to do. For each, give: type, the EXACT sentence quoted word for word, the person, and any date or deadline stated. Rules: - Quote; never paraphrase or tidy the sentence. - If a commitment or request is implied rather than stated ("will look into it", "would be good to have"), include it and mark it "implicit?". - If a message has none, write "none found" for that message. Do not invent one. - When unsure, include it. I will prune. [paste ten sent messages, each with its date and recipient]

Once the list exists, turning its dates into reminders, or a "Waiting for" line into an automatic nudge, is workflow plumbing; that is Life and Work Automation ↗.

Real world

The contract that was "in the email" (constructed). An ops lead replies to a supplier at the bottom of a long thread: "Thanks, I will get the signed copy back to you once legal has looked." No date, no task created, and the message is now three replies deep under newer ones. Three weeks later the supplier chases, politely, and the lead has no memory of promising anything. A sweep of the sent folder would have caught the sentence, marked it "implicit?" because no date was stated, and put it on the list with a question to answer: by when?

Part 3 · covers why writing it down helps, and the disagreement

Why the list helps, and what is still argued

5 min live

You will have heard that unfinished tasks nag at you until you write them down, and that writing them down makes the nagging stop. Two serious pieces of research bear on that claim, and they point in different directions because they measured different things.

LiveWhat each study found, in its own words3 min▶

Masicampo and Baumeister (2011, JPSP) gave people unfinished goals and measured the cost. Unfinished goals produced intrusive thoughts during an unrelated task, high accessibility of goal-related words, and poorer anagram performance. Then the key manipulation, in the abstract's words: "Allowing participants to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals eliminated the various activation and interference effects." Not finishing the goal: planning it.

Ghibellini and Meier (2025) pooled the research on two old claims. The Zeigarnik effect says interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones. The Ovsiankina effect says people tend to go back to interrupted tasks. Their summary: "We found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks but found a general tendency to resume tasks." The weighted recall ratio, interrupted to completed, was 0.99 across 38 publications. They conclude the Zeigarnik effect "lacks universal validity", while the Ovsiankina resumption tendency is robust.

Self-studyTeaching the disagreement, not resolving it3 min▶

It is tempting to stitch these together into one story: unfinished tasks haunt memory, a plan lays them to rest. The 2025 meta-analysis takes out the memory half of that story; the 2011 study never measured recall in the first place. They measured different outcomes: one asked whether unfinished goals intrude on attention and stay mentally active, the other asked whether unfinished tasks are better remembered. A goal can stay active without being better recalled. Whether the intrusion and accessibility effects of 2011 hold up across many labs the way recall was tested in 2025 is not something either paper answers, so it is left open here.

The practical case for the list does not need that question settled. It rests on two things that do not depend on it. Memory is not a reliable store for promises: no study above shows that unfinished tasks are reliably remembered, and the 0.99 ratio says they are not remembered better. People tend to resume interrupted tasks, which the meta-analysis finds robust: a list with a sentence and a date gives that tendency something to resume from. If writing a specific plan also quiets the background noise, as the 2011 study found in its setting, that is a bonus, not the foundation.

Real world

The colleague who says "I never forget a promise" (constructed). A team lead keeps no list and says the open loops "nag at me until they are done". Some do. The ones that did not nag are, by definition, the ones nobody hears about until the other side chases. A feeling of being nagged is evidence that some loops are active, not that all of them are. The list is not a cure for a nagging brain; it is a record that does not depend on which loops happened to nag this week.

Build-along

Ten sent messages, one honest list

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You build the list from your own sent mail, with an assistant doing the reading and you doing the deciding. The pipeline is extract, check, add what was missed, date. Expect the assistant to suggest some lines you will reject and to miss at least one you will find yourself; that is the design working, not failing.

Pick ten sent messages from the last fortnight. Not your ten most important: the ten most recent that went to a person rather than a list. Copy each with its date and recipient. Strip anything you would not paste into the assistant your organisation has approved.

★ Step 1 · the sample, written downSample: last 10 sent messages to a person, newest first Window: 21 Sep to 2 Oct Skipped: 2 calendar replies, 1 message with a payroll attachment (not pasted) Format per message: date | to | new text only

Run the extraction prompt from Part 2. Paste all ten in one go. You want every candidate, including the implied ones; resist the urge to tell it to "only list the important ones".

★ Step 2 · run it[the extraction prompt from Part 2, unchanged] [message 1] 2 Oct | to Ana | Thanks, I will send the revised plan Friday. [message 2] 28 Sep | to Lee | Can you send the signed invoice when you get a minute? ... [message 10] 2 Oct | to Raj | Will look into it and come back to you.

Check each candidate against its sentence. Open the quoted sentence in the original message. Keep it if you really promised or really asked. Reject it if it is pleasantry ("let me know if you have questions"), already done, or someone else's ask in a quoted reply. Every "implicit?" line gets a decision: firm it up or strike it.

★ Step 3 · the check, one line per candidateKEEP "I will send the revised plan Friday." commitment, Ana KEEP "Can you send the signed invoice ..." request, Lee REJECT "Let me know if you have questions." pleasantry, not a promise KEEP "Will look into it and come back to you." implicit? -> yes, I owe Raj an answer MISSED (msg 6) "I'll loop in finance next week." found on reread, commitment

Add a due date and the person to every kept line. Where the sentence states a date, use it. Where it does not ("come back to you"), set one now and write it down; a promise without a date is the kind that slips. Then reread the ten messages yourself, quickly, for anything the assistant missed.

★ Step 4 · the "I owe" columnI OWE - Ana | "I will send the revised plan Friday." | due Fri 9 Oct - Raj | "Will look into it and come back to you." | due Tue 6 Oct (my date) - Finance via Sam | "I'll loop in finance next week." | due Fri 9 Oct

Build the "Waiting for" column from your own requests. Every request you made is a line with a chase date: the date you asked for, or a few working days after you asked if you gave none.

★ Step 5 · the "Waiting for" columnWAITING FOR - Lee | "Can you send the signed invoice ...?" | asked Mon 28 Sep | chase Thu 1 Oct (overdue) - Priya | "Could you confirm the venue by the 8th?" | asked Wed 30 Sep | chase Thu 8 Oct

Set one review date. Pick a time this week, in the calendar, to go down both columns: tick what landed, chase what is late, renegotiate what you cannot meet. One date now; session 6 turns it into a weekly habit with its own checklist.

★ Step 6 · the review, bookedCalendar: Fri 9 Oct, 16:30, 20 min, "Open loops" Agenda: tick received, chase overdue Waiting For, renegotiate any I owe I cannot meet, add this week's sent mail (another ten messages)

Count your own pipeline. Write down how many candidates the assistant gave, how many you kept, how many you rejected, and how many you found by hand. Those four numbers are your own extraction precision and recall, on your own mail, and they are what you bring to session 4.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Three annotators marking Enron emails for requests agreed at kappa 0.681. What does that mean for any extraction of requests or commitments from your mail?

Kappa measures agreement between annotators beyond what chance would give. 0.681 is solid but far from perfect: trained readers with a written definition still split on a real share of messages. The share of unanimously agreed messages containing a request was a different number, 52.08 percent.

2 · How do the 2011 study by Masicampo and Baumeister and the 2025 meta-analysis by Ghibellini and Meier relate?

One measured intrusion, accessibility and interference; the other pooled recall studies. They do not cancel each other out, and neither settles "your brain nags you". The practical case for the list rests on not trusting memory and on the resumption tendency.

3 · Why ask the assistant to over-extract and then prune the list yourself?

The two errors do not cost the same. Extraction is a recall job; the human check is the precision pass. The honesty clause (quote, mark "implicit?", say "none found") keeps the over-extraction checkable.
