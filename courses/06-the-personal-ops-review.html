The personal ops review

Five sessions built the parts: a log of where the week goes, triage rules scored against a base rate, an open-loops list, a bench, and a permission slip for every assistant that touches your mail. Parts drift. Rules that scored well in October flag the wrong things by February, a waiting-for line quietly ages, an assistant does one thing its slip never allowed. Tonight is the half hour a week that notices, with an assistant doing the sweeping and you doing every decision, and a one-page spec that writes the whole system down.

Part 0

Where we left off

Session 5 ended with an audit: every app that can touch your mailbox, its scope in plain words, and a one-line permission slip for each assistant you kept. A slip is a decision made once. Everything else in the system is the same: a rule chosen once, a list started once, a log run once. Tonight turns each of those one-off decisions into something that gets looked at again on a schedule, because the failure every session has warned about is the silent one, and silence is only broken by someone looking.

Part 1 · covers the weekly review, adapted for ops

Get clear, get current, then decide

6 min live

The best-known weekly review is the one in the David Allen Company's GTD Weekly Review checklist. It is a practitioner method, a checklist refined in practice, not a study, and nothing here claims evidence that it works. It earns its place because its three stages map cleanly onto the loops from session 1, and because it already separates the work of finding things from the work of deciding about them. That separation is exactly where an assistant fits. The notes half of a weekly review, your reading and your ideas, is taught in PKM, session 5 ↗; here the review is about mail, commitments and agents.

LiveThe ops review in 30 minutes, step by step4 min▶

The split below is a suggested one, not a measured one; your first two reviews will tell you where it runs long. The order matters more than the minutes: clear first, so you are deciding about a complete picture.

0 to 5, get clear. Run your triage rules from session 2 one last time for the week and take the inbox to zero in the GTD sense: not answered, decided. Each message is either acted on now, added to a list, or archived. Pull anything written on paper or in chat into the same place.

5 to 10, read the sweep. The assistant's sweep, run before you sat down, lists candidate open loops from last week's received and sent mail, each with the sentence it came from. You read it as a precision pass, exactly as in session 3: keep what is real, strike what is not, and never add a line the sweep invented.

10 to 20, get current. Walk the "I owe" column, then the "Waiting for" column, then last week's calendar for promises made in meetings, then next week's calendar for anything that needs preparing. For each waiting-for line older than a week, the assistant may have a nudge drafted. You decide whether it goes, and you send it.

20 to 25, the agent check. Part 2: each assistant's log against its slip, five actions each.

25 to 30, get creative, then decide. Someday/maybe, new ideas, and the one decision the review exists for: which three things matter next week. That sentence is yours and does not get drafted.

Self-studyWhy the review, not the tools, is what stops leaks3 min▶

Look back at the constructed week in session 1. Triage, reply and chase all had minutes against them. Review had zero. That was a constructed example, but the shape is worth taking seriously, because it shows how a system can leak even when every part works. Rules catch the messages they were written for. Extraction catches the commitments that look like commitments. Neither can notice its own misses: a rule that stopped matching, a promise made on a call, a waiting-for line nobody re-read. Only a pass that looks at the whole list, on a schedule, can see what nothing caught.

Session 3 explained why the list itself is worth keeping: people do tend to resume interrupted tasks, and writing a plan down is a better bet than relying on memory. A list nobody reads has neither benefit. The review is what turns the list from an archive into a working tool.

Real world

A checklist written for paper that already had a Waiting For line. The GTD Weekly Review checklist predates today's AI assistants, and its "get current" stage already names "Review Waiting For List" as its own step, separate from your action lists. The split session 3 drew between "I owe" and "Waiting for" is not new; the checklist already treats what other people owe you as a list of its own, reviewed every week. What is new is that the sweep for it can be delegated. The checking cannot.

Part 2 · covers reviewing what agents did

Read the log before you trust the agent

5 min live

A permission slip says what an assistant may do. A log says what it did. The weekly agent check puts the two side by side, spot-checks five actions, and ends in one of three decisions. It takes five minutes per assistant, and it is the only point in the system where a scope granted in session 5 meets evidence of how it was used.

LiveFive actions, three questions3 min▶

Anthropic's own computer-use documentation is blunt about this: "Always carefully review and verify Claude's computer use actions and logs." The advice generalises to any assistant with access to your mail. Reading a whole week of actions is not realistic. Five is, and five spread across the week see more of it than five from Monday.

For each of the five, ask three questions. Is it inside the slip? Compare the action's rung, read, label, draft, send or delete, with what the slip allows. Would I have done the same? A label you would have applied differently is a quality problem; note it for the monthly re-score. Could I undo it? Anything you could not undo, a sent message, a deletion, a forwarded thread, gets a second look even if it was technically allowed. Also look once at the standing rules session 5 warned about: a filter the assistant created keeps acting after the log goes quiet.

Self-studyWhen the log shows an action outside its slip2 min▶

Noting it is not a decision. If the slip says "send: no" and the log shows a send, then either the scope it holds allows more than the slip says, or something outside your instructions told it to act. Session 5 showed both happen. In both cases the fix sits at the permission, not in a reminder to yourself. Narrow means re-granting with fewer scopes, or moving the assistant down the ladder from session 5. Revoke means removing its access from your account's connected-apps page today and deciding later whether to grant it again.

Where those pages are and what they show changes with each product release. The steps were read in October 2026; re-check them in your own account before relying on them.

Self-studyNo log you can read, no delegation past draft2 min▶

Some assistants keep a clear history of what they did; some keep nothing you can see. If you cannot read the log, you cannot run the check, and an assistant you cannot check should stay at a rung where you see every result anyway: read, label, or draft. How agent builders design logging and observability in the first place, and why, is architecture, taught in AI Agents, session a4 ↗. From your chair the rule is simpler: the log is a precondition for trust, not a bonus feature.

Real world

The vendor tells you to read the log. Anthropic's computer-use documentation advises "Always carefully review and verify Claude's computer use actions and logs." The red-team results and the "mailbox hygiene" email from Anthropic's own Claude in Chrome pilot, both in session 5, show why: an instruction hidden in a message can move an agent to act, and the place you would find out is the log. A weekly five-action check is the smallest version of that advice that a person will actually keep doing.

Part 3 · covers keeping rules honest over time

Re-score the rules on fresh labels

4 min live

A rule scored on the bench in session 4 was scored on three mailboxes from 2001. A rule scored on your own mail in October was scored on October. Senders change, projects end, a newsletter starts writing "please" in every subject. The only number worth trusting about a rule is a recent one on your own labels, so once a month you label one fresh week and count again.

LiveWhy last month's precision is not this month's3 min▶

Two things move under a rule. The base rate moves: on the session 4 bench the share of mail acted on was 25.9 percent across all three mailboxes, but 12.3 percent for Corman, 30.8 for Keiser and 34.6 for Heard. A precision number means nothing until it sits next to the base rate of the same mail, and your base rate in a quiet month is not your base rate in a launch month. The cost of a mistake moves too, and differs from person to person: a missed message costs one person an awkward apology and another a lost contract.

Gmail's Priority Inbox engineers wrote down the second point in 2010: they tuned a per-user threshold because "users do not agree on the cost of a false positive versus a false negative". On 160,000 messages users had marked by hand, error was 45 percent with one global model, 38 with per-user models, and 31 with per-user models and per-user thresholds. A personal rule is a per-user threshold you set by hand. It needs the same thing theirs did: your labels, recent ones.

LiveThe cadence: four loops at four speeds2 min▶
WhenWhatTime
DailyTriage passes with your rules, a few times a day rather than continuouslyMinutes, spread out
WeeklyThe ops review: clear, current, agent check, decide30 min, same slot
MonthlyRe-score every rule on one fresh labelled week; check each assistant's scope still matches its jobAbout an hour
QuarterlyRe-run the five-day log from session 1 and compare it with the first oneFive days, a few minutes each

The quarterly log is the only honest answer to "did any of this give me time back". Two logs, months apart, by loop. If the ops share did not move, the system is costing you as much as it saves, and the review is where you say so.

Self-studyRetiring a rule without guilt2 min▶

A rule whose precision has drifted down to the base rate is now flagging mail at random with respect to what you act on: lift near 1.00, the same reading the session 4 bench gave the "from my company" rule. Keeping it costs attention and buys nothing. Retire it, write the date and the last counts in the spec, and move on.

Be careful with small counts before you retire anything. On the bench, the "subject says urgent" rung flagged five messages, and a shuffle of the labels pushed it to 60 percent precision by chance. A rule that flagged five messages this month can swing by twenty points on one message. Give a low-volume rule two or three months of counts before you judge it.

Real world

Gmail did not trust one threshold for everyone. Priority Inbox, described by its engineers at a NIPS workshop in 2010, learned importance from how each user handled mail after delivery: opens, replies, corrections. Even with that, a single global model got 45 percent of user-marked messages wrong; adding per-user models and per-user thresholds brought it to 31. If they had to personalise, a rule you wrote once for your own inbox deserves at least a monthly look at your own counts.

Build-along

Your one-page personal-ops spec

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Everything from six sessions fits on one sheet. Writing it down does two jobs: it shows the gaps (a loop with no owner, an assistant with no slip, a rule with no counts), and it gives the weekly review something fixed to check against. Fill in what you have tonight; the brackets you cannot fill yet are your to-do list.

Copy the template. Plain text, anywhere you will open every Friday. Square brackets are the parts that change. Fill what you can from sessions 1 to 5 now; leave the rest bracketed.

★ Step 1 · the spec templateMY PERSONAL-OPS SPEC - [name] - updated [date] 1. LOOPS AND OWNERS triage: [me / rules / assistant name] [how often] reply: [me, drafts by assistant name?] chase: [me, nudges drafted by ...] review: me, [day and time] 2. RULES (last re-score: [date]) [rule name] [search string] flagged [n] hits [n] misses [n] 3. ASSISTANTS AND SLIPS [app] scope: [scope string] may: [read/label/draft] never: [send/delete] last log check: [date] 4. OPEN-LOOPS LIST lives in: [one place] columns: I owe / Waiting for 5. CADENCE daily triage [times] / weekly review [day] / monthly re-score [date] quarterly five-day log [month] 6. NEVER DELEGATE [the decisions and messages that stay mine]

Write the weekly sweep prompt. This is the assistant's whole job in the review: find candidates, quote the sentence, invent nothing, send nothing. Run it before you sit down, then read the result as a precision pass.

★ Step 2 · the weekly-review sweepBelow are the messages I sent and received between [Monday date] and [Friday date]. List every open loop you can find, in two groups: I OWE: something I asked for, agreed to, or promised. WAITING FOR: something I asked someone else for that has no reply yet. For each line give: the person, the exact sentence it came from (quoted), and the date. If you find none in a group, write "none found". Do not invent a loop, a person or a date. Do not draft replies. Do not send, archive, label or delete anything. [paste or attach last week's messages]

Write the agent log checklist. One block per assistant, copied into your review notes each Friday. It ends in a decision, not a note.

★ Step 3 · the agent log checkASSISTANT: [name] SLIP: [read / label / draft; never send / delete] [ ] Opened its log or history for the last 7 days [ ] Picked 5 actions, spread across the week [ ] Each one inside the slip? [yes / no: which] [ ] Would I have done the same? [yes / differently: how] [ ] Anything I could not undo? [none / what] [ ] Any new filter or standing rule? [none / what] DECISION: [keep / narrow the scope to ... / revoke today]

Set up the monthly re-score sheet. Label one recent week as act, read or archive by what you actually did, exactly as on the session 4 bench. Then count each rule. Precision is hits over flagged; recall is hits over hits plus misses; the base rate is acted-on messages over all messages that week. The rows below are constructed to show the arithmetic.

Precision near the base rate: retire. Three flagged: too few to judge, wait another month.

Put the review in your calendar. A recurring 30-minute block, same day and time every week, named after the job it does so that it reads as work when someone tries to book over it. Add a monthly one for the re-score and a quarterly reminder for the five-day log.

★ Step 5 · the recurring blocksWeekly, Friday [time], 30 min: "Ops review: loops, agent log, next week's three" Monthly, first Monday, 60 min: "Rule re-score + scope check" Quarterly, first week: "Five-day log, round [n]"

Section 6 is the one you do not get to skip. The never-delegate line is what stays yours however capable the assistant becomes. Which kinds of message and decision belong on it is taught in AI Office, session 6 ↗; write your own version of it here, in your own words, and read it at every review.

Homework

The last one, and it takes four weeks

30 min a week, for 4 weeks
  1. Run the review on four Fridays in a row. Same slot, same order: clear, sweep, current, agent check, decide. Note where it ran long.
  2. Re-score once, at the end of the month. Label one fresh week, fill the sheet from step 4, and write the counts into section 2 of your spec.
  3. Re-run the five-day log in a quarter. Put it in the calendar now. Compare the ops share with your first log from session 1.
  4. Revoke one scope you no longer need. There is almost always one: an app tried once, a grant above its job. Remove it, and update section 3.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and the passport is full.

1 · In the weekly ops review, which of these is the assistant's job rather than yours?

Finding is a recall job, and an assistant can do it. Deciding what matters, accepting or dropping a commitment, and sending anything all stay with you. The GTD checklist already separates the sweep from the decisions; the assistant only takes the sweep.

2 · Your triage rule scored well on the session 4 bench. Why re-score it every month on your own fresh labels?

The bench itself showed base rates of 12.3, 30.8 and 34.6 percent in three mailboxes. Gmail's engineers tuned a per-user threshold because "users do not agree on the cost of a false positive versus a false negative". Your rule needs your labels, and recent ones.

3 · The weekly agent check shows your assistant sent a message, and its permission slip says "send: no". What do you do?

An action outside the slip means the platform's scope allows more than you decided, or something told the assistant to act. Either way the fix sits at the permission, not in a note or a polite instruction. Narrow it or pull it.
