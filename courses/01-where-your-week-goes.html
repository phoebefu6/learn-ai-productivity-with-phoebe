Where your week actually goes

Every pitch for an AI assistant starts with a number about your week: a quarter of it lost to email, an interruption every two minutes. Both numbers are real, both come from serious sources, and both describe someone who is probably not you. Tonight we read the small print on each, then do the thing almost nobody does before automating anything: count where your own week goes, for five days, by the loop it belongs to. The log decides which session of the next five matters most to you.

Part 0

Where we start

Personal ops is the work around the work: deciding what to look at, answering, chasing what you are owed, keeping track of what you owe, and once a week checking that nothing slipped. It is the part of a job most worth handing to software, because it repeats, and the part where handing it over badly costs the most, because the failure is silent. Six sessions build one system for it, measured at every step.

Part 1 · covers the numbers everyone quotes

Two famous numbers, and their small print

6 min live

"Knowledge workers spend 28 percent of their week on email." You have seen it on a slide. It comes from a 184-page McKinsey Global Institute report from July 2012, and the sentence in the report is narrower than the slide, older than the slide, and built differently from what the slide implies.

LiveWhat the 28 percent sentence actually says4 min▶

Page 46 of "The social economy" reads, in full: "Typically, such a worker spends 65 percent of a workday collaborating and communicating with others. This includes 28 percent of work time reading, writing, or responding to e-mail, and 19 percent of working hours trying to track down information needed to complete tasks."

Three things are in the small print. "Such a worker" is an interaction worker, McKinsey's term for high-skill knowledge workers whose roles rely on communication, picked out by occupation code. It is not every employee. The source, in footnote 60, is "McKinsey Global Institute analysis based on proprietary McKinsey data and Susan Feldman, Hidden cost of information work: A progress report, International Data Corporation, May 2009." The appendix says the IDC numbers came from surveys of how workers spend their time. The arithmetic in Exhibit A7: 13.0 hours of email in an average week of 46.5 hours. The ten IDC activities in the same exhibit add up to 65.8 hours, so people were doing several at once, and McKinsey rescaled them into four buckets.

None of that makes the number wrong. It makes it a survey-based estimate for one kind of worker in 2009, which is a different thing from "you spend a quarter of your week on email". The report's cover adds to the confusion by printing "28 hours", which is email, searching and internal collaboration added together.

Live"Interrupted every two minutes", and whose two minutes3 min▶

Microsoft's Work Trend Index special report of June 2025, "Breaking down the infinite workday", is built on Microsoft 365 telemetry rather than surveys, which makes it far better evidence of what happens. It reports that the average worker receives 117 emails a day, "most of them skimmed in under 60 seconds", and 153 Teams messages per weekday.

The headline that travelled is "interrupted every two minutes, 275 times a day". The methodology note at the bottom of the same page defines it: a ping is a meeting invite, email or chat received; the two minutes is the average gap between pings in an eight-hour day; the 275 counts the 24-hour day; and the figure is "Based on the top 20% of users by ping volume received." It describes the busiest fifth of users, and it counts arrivals, not interruptions. Whether a ping interrupts you depends on whether you let it, which is what session 2 is about.

Self-studyWhy a lab speed-up is not a week saved2 min▶

The gap between "40 percent faster on one task" and "a few percent of a real week" is taught in full, with Microsoft's own randomized trial, in AI Office, session 1 ↗. The short version you need here: a speed-up only applies to the slice of the week the tool can reach, and you cannot know the size of that slice without measuring it. That measurement is tonight's build-along.

Real world

The slide that said "28 percent of all workers". A vendor deck takes the McKinsey sentence, drops "interaction worker", drops 2012, and multiplies 28 percent by a whole company's payroll to price the email problem. Every step of the multiplication is arithmetic and the first step is a scope error. Ask for the denominator: whose week, measured how, in what year. If the answer is "it is a well-known statistic", the number has been copied more often than it has been read.

Part 2 · covers the five-day log

Measure before you automate

5 min live

The fix for a borrowed number is your own number. Not a survey of how you think you spend your time, which is what IDC asked people and why its rows summed to more than the week, but a log written as the week happens, sorted into the loops that personal ops is made of.

LiveThe six buckets, and why four of them are "ops"3 min▶

triage: deciding what to look at. Scanning the inbox, the chat badges, the notifications. Nothing is answered yet. reply: answering what needs an answer. chase: following up on what you are owed, and keeping track of what you owe. review: the deliberate pass, daily or weekly, that checks nothing slipped. Those four are personal ops.

meetings and the work itself are logged too, because a share means nothing without the whole. If the work itself is the biggest bucket, good: that is the bucket to protect, not to automate. Meeting time is a calendar problem, which Data PMO, practitioner session 7 ↗ covers.

Self-studyHow to log without the log becoming the job3 min▶

Write it as it happens, not at the end of the day. End-of-day recall is the survey problem again: people remember the meeting and forget the twelve two-minute inbox checks. A sticky note with six tally columns works. So does a note on your phone with one line per switch.

Fifteen-minute resolution is enough. You are looking for which bucket is large, not for precision to the minute. Five working days, not one. Monday and Friday are different animals. Count switches as well as minutes if you can: a column of tallies each time you went to the inbox. The Work Trend Index counted pings received; you are counting the ones you answered, which is the number you control.

Part 3 · covers which loop to hand over first

Frequent, rule-shaped, and cheap to get wrong

4 min live

The log tells you where the time is. It does not tell you what to hand over. Three questions do, in order, and the order matters because the third one is the one people skip.

LiveWhy reversibility outranks time saved3 min▶

A wrong label costs you a second to fix and nobody else ever sees it. A wrong sent message is read before you notice. A wrong deletion may not be noticed at all. The minutes a loop costs you decide whether it is worth automating; whether its mistakes can be undone decides how much of it you hand over. Triage labelling usually passes all three questions. Replying usually fails the third, which is why the default for replies is a draft, not a send.

The plumbing for loops that pass, the triggers and the no-code workflows, is its own subject: Life and Work Automation ↗. Here the job is choosing which loop, and measuring whether the handover worked.

Real world

The demo picks the wrong loop. Assistant demos almost always show drafting a reply, because it looks impressive on a screen. A real five-day log usually shows triage, many short visits to the inbox, as the most frequent loop, and chasing as the one that silently costs the most when it fails. Neither demos well. Both are where sessions 2 to 4 start.

Build-along

Your five-day log, and the first loop to work on

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Set up the log now, run the first entry tonight, and use the counter below to read it. The counter is pre-filled with the constructed week from Part 2; replace it with your own lines as the week goes.

Make the log. Six columns, one per bucket. Paper, a note, or a sheet. The format matters less than keeping it within reach for five days.

★ Step 1 · the log, one line per switchMon 08:40 triage 15 inbox + chat badges, nothing answered Mon 08:55 the work 70 forecast model Mon 10:05 reply 20 3 answers, 1 parked Mon 10:25 meetings 60 weekly ops Mon 11:25 chase 10 nudged Sam on the contract ...

Let an assistant do the tally, not the remembering. At the end of each day paste your raw lines in and ask for totals by bucket. It is adding up what you wrote, which is a task it is good at, and the honesty clause stops it filling gaps.

★ Step 2 · the daily tallyHere is today's time log, one line per switch: time, bucket, minutes, note. Add up the minutes per bucket and give me a table: bucket, minutes, share of the logged total. Use only the lines I gave you. If a line has no minutes or no bucket, list it under "unreadable" and do not guess. [paste today's lines]

After five days, put the totals in the counter above and read the largest bucket and the ops share. Then run each ops bucket through the three questions from Part 3.

Write one sentence. "My first loop is ___, because it happens ___ times a day, I can / cannot write the rule, and a mistake can / cannot be undone." That sentence decides which of sessions 2 to 6 you should not skip.

★ Step 4 · the decision, written downFirst loop: triage Frequency: 14 inbox visits a day (from my tallies) Rule: partly - "to me, from a person, short list" covers most of it Undo: yes - a wrong label costs one click So: hand over the labelling, review it weekly. Sessions 2 and 4 first.

Keep the five-day log. Session 6 asks you to run it again a month from now. Two logs a month apart are the only honest answer to "did the system save me any time".

Homework

Before session 2

10 min a day
  1. Run the log for five working days. As it happens, fifteen-minute resolution, six buckets.
  2. Tally your inbox visits on at least two of the days. One tick each time you open the inbox or a chat badge.
  3. Find one number about your week that someone has quoted at you, and trace it to the sentence it came from. Write down whose week it describes and what year.
  4. Bring your largest ops bucket and your one-sentence decision. Session 2 starts from triage, because for most people that is where the visits are.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What does McKinsey's 2012 "28 percent of work time on email" actually describe?

Footnote 60 names the sources and the appendix explains the split. The cover's "28 hours" is email, search and internal collaboration added together, a different number. Neither is a measurement of you.

2 · Microsoft's 2025 report says employees are "interrupted every two minutes, 275 times a day". What does its methodology note say that figure is based on?

It is telemetry, which is good evidence, about the busiest fifth of users, and it counts messages arriving, not interruptions. Whether a ping interrupts you depends on whether you answer it.

3 · Your log shows replying is a daily loop and you can write a rule for most of it. Why might it still not be the first loop to hand over completely?

Frequency and rule-shape say a loop is worth working on. Reversibility decides how much of it you hand over. Labels pass; sends do not. Session 5 turns that into the permission an assistant is actually granted.
