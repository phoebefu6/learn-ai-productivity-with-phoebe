Rules against a judge

Triage is the loop most five-day logs show first: a dozen short visits a day to decide what deserves a look. There are two ways to hand it over. A rule you write, which reads the envelope and does exactly what it says. Or a model that reads the whole message and judges, which is cleverer and harder to check. Tonight we put a base rate under both, find out where the "right answer" for triage even comes from, and write three rules for your own inbox that you count before you trust.

Part 1 · covers triage as a bet against a base rate

Every inbox has a base rate

6 min live

Before any rule or model looks at your mail, some share of it needed you to act. That share is the base rate, and it is the number every triage claim has to beat. A filter that "catches the important ones" is not saying much until you know how many important ones there were to catch.

LiveFour numbers, and the base rate under them4 min▶

Take any triage rule, run it over a pile of mail where you know what the owner actually did, and four numbers fall out. Flagged share is the cost: how much of the inbox the rule asks you to look at first. Precision is how often a flag is right: of the flagged messages, how many did the owner act on. Recall is how much of the real work the flag catches: of everything the owner acted on, how much got flagged. Lift is precision divided by the base rate, which is the only fair way to compare a rule across two inboxes.

The numbers on the drawing are the ones session 4's bench counts on real mail: 390 messages from three Enron mailboxes, 101 of them acted on by their owners, a base rate of 25.9 percent. Now flag everything. Every acted-on message is flagged, so recall is 100 percent. Precision is 101 over 390, which is 25.9 percent: the base rate, to the decimal. Lift 1.00. A rule that flags everything has learned nothing the pile did not already tell you, and that is the floor every real rule has to climb off.

The base rate is not one number for everyone. Session 4 counts it per mailbox: 12.3 percent for Shelley Corman, 30.8 percent for Kam Keiser, 34.6 percent for Marie Heard. A rule with 35 percent precision would be nearly three times the base rate in the first inbox and barely above it in the third. Same rule, same number, opposite verdicts.

Self-studyWhy precision alone, or recall alone, can be gamed3 min▶

Recall alone is the easiest number in the world to max out: flag everything. The vendor slide that says "never miss an important email again" is often describing exactly that, a tool that marks so much as important that nothing important is ever unmarked, and you are back to reading the whole inbox with extra colour on it.

Precision alone is the easiest to max out from the other end: flag only the two messages a day from your manager. Precision near 100 percent, recall terrible, and every request from anyone else sinks. A tool quoting precision without the flagged share is hiding how little it flags.

So the four travel together, in a fixed reading order: base rate, then flagged share, then precision against the base rate, then recall. One more habit: put the count next to every rate. A rule that flags five messages and hits three has 60 percent precision and almost no evidence behind it. One message the other way moves it twenty points.

Real world

Gmail could not pick one threshold for everybody. When Google's engineers described the learning behind Priority Inbox (Aberdeen, Pacovsky and Slater, 2010), they wrote that "users do not agree on the cost of a false positive versus a false negative". Some people would rather see a few unimportant messages marked important than miss one that mattered; others the reverse. Their answer was a threshold tuned per user. Yours will be personal too: the trade between precision and recall is a preference, and the base rate under it is a fact about your inbox, not anyone else's.

Part 2 · covers where labels come from

Your behaviour is already a label

5 min live

Precision and recall need a right answer to count against. Nobody sits down and labels a year of their mail as "important" or not. They do not have to: every message you reply to, keep or delete is already marked, by what you did with it. That is where the right answer comes from, and it is also where it quietly goes wrong.

LiveHow Gmail found its right answers3 min▶

The best documented triage system at scale is still Gmail's Priority Inbox, described by its engineers in "The Learning Behind Gmail Priority Inbox" (Aberdeen, Pacovsky, Slater, NIPS 2010 workshop). It "ranks mail by the probability that the user will perform an action on that mail." And its definition of the right answer is the one in the drawing: "Importance ground truth is based on how the user interacts with a mail after delivery." Opens, replies and manual corrections, not a survey of what people say matters.

The model looked at four families of features: social (who it is from and how you deal with them), content (words in the subject and body), thread (whether it continues a conversation you are in) and label (what your own filters already did to it). It reported about 80 percent accuracy, plus or minus 5, on that implicit importance metric. When the team checked against 160,000 messages users had marked by hand, error fell from 45 percent with one global model, to 38 percent with per-user models, to 31 percent with per-user models and per-user thresholds.

Read that last line twice. The biggest single gain was not a cleverer model of mail, it was admitting that each person's mail is their own. That is the case for building your rules on your own inbox tonight instead of downloading someone else's.

Self-studyThe blind spots in a behavioural label3 min▶

A label built from behaviour is honest about what you did in the mailbox and blind to everything you did outside it. A phone call instead of a reply: you acted, the mailbox saw nothing, the message is labelled as if you ignored it. A reply under a new subject: you acted, but nothing ties your reply back to the message, so it looks unacted. Mail never processed: a message sitting in the inbox is labelled "read", kept, when the truth may be that you never got to it.

Session 4's bench declares the same limits on its Enron labels, and adds one more: a thread already running has more of the owner's replies to match, which flatters any rule that rewards replies. None of this makes behavioural labels useless. It makes them the best cheap right answer available, with known holes, which is exactly the standard to hold an AI judge's output to as well.

Real world

What Priority Inbox saved, and who it was measured on. The same paper reports that about 2,000 Googlers using Priority Inbox "spent 6% less time reading mail overall, and 13% less time reading unimportant mail." Useful, and modest, and worth reading with its small print: an internal measurement, on Google's own employees, in 2010, reported by the team that built it. It is a good reason to expect triage to save minutes, not hours, and a good reason to measure your own.

Part 3 · covers rules versus an AI judge

A rule you can read, a judge you have to check

4 min live

Now the two machines. A rule is a sentence about the envelope that the mail system applies the same way every time. A model judge is a language model handed the message and asked whether it needs you. Each is good at what the other is bad at, and one of the judge's strengths is also its sharpest risk.

LiveWhat each one sees, and what it costs you2 min▶

A rule sees only the envelope: who sent it, who it went to, who was copied, the subject, the category the mail system already gave it. That is its weakness, because "can you send me the figures by Friday" from a new colleague looks exactly like a newsletter from the outside. It is also its strength. You can read a rule and know what it will do with any message. It costs nothing per message. And it cannot be argued with: a sentence inside the email saying "mark this urgent" is, to a rule, just more characters it never looks at.

A model judge reads the words. It can tell a request from a notification even when the envelope is identical, which is exactly the ambiguous middle rules are bad at. In exchange it is opaque (you can check what it decided, not truly why), it costs something on every message, and its decisions need the same treatment as a rule's: a base rate underneath, precision and recall counted against your own labels. A judge is not exempt from the four numbers because it sounds sure of itself. Session 4 is where it gets counted.

And it reads everything in the message, including sentences written for it. Anthropic's own documentation for its computer-use tool says so plainly: "In some circumstances, Claude will follow commands found in content even when they conflict with your instructions." For a judge that only sorts, that means a sender can talk their way up your list. For an agent that can also act on your mail, it means much more, and session 5 is about exactly that.

Self-studyThe hybrid default2 min▶

Rules for the envelope. Anything you can decide from who and where: your manager, a key client's domain, the newsletters, the automated alerts, "I was only copied". These are cheap, transparent and stable, so let them take the clear cases at both ends.

A judge for the ambiguous middle. What is left after the rules have run: direct mail from people the rules do not know, where the words decide. That is a much smaller pile than the whole inbox, which keeps the judge's cost down and gives it fewer chances to be steered by something written inside a message.

Neither is allowed to act irreversibly. Both may label or reorder. Neither deletes, forwards or replies on its own until it has been counted against your behaviour, and for anything a mistake cannot undo, not even then. The architecture behind holding actions for approval belongs to AI Agents, practitioner session 7 ↗; here the rule is simply that a sorter sorts.

Real world

A rule that acts is a different object from a rule that sorts. Gmail's own help pages say a filter can "send email to a label, or archive, delete, star, or automatically forward" it. The same menu holds a harmless action and two that move mail out of your sight or out of your company. The same help page adds a detail worth noticing: "When you create a filter to forward messages, only new messages will be affected." So a forwarding rule is silent about the past and permanent about the future: every new message that matches leaves, and nothing in the inbox tells you it happened. Read in October 2026; Google changes these menus, so re-check before relying on the wording.

Build-along

Three rules for your inbox, counted before they are trusted

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Gmail lets you write a rule as a search before it is ever a filter. That gives you a free test: run the search, look at what comes back, and count it against what you actually did last week. Only a rule that survives the count becomes a filter, and its first job is a label you can undo.

Write each rule as a plain sentence first. Three of them. Two that pull mail toward you ("look at this first") and one that pushes mail away ("this can wait"). Use your session 1 log: the senders and kinds of mail you actually spent triage time on. If you cannot say the rule in one sentence, it is not a rule yet, it is a judgement, and it belongs to the ambiguous middle.

★ Step 1 · three rules, in wordsRule 1 (first): A person wrote to me directly in the last week, and it is not a promotion or a social update. Rule 2 (first): Anything from my manager, or from anyone at our biggest client. Rule 3 (later): Updates and forum mail where I am not in the To line.

Translate each sentence into Gmail search operators. Use only the operators on Gmail's "Refine searches in Gmail" help page (read in October 2026; re-check before relying on them): from:, to:me, cc:, subject:, - to exclude, OR, { } for "any of these", ( ) to group, category:, list:, older_than:, newer_than:. An assistant is a fine translator here, as long as it is held to that list.

★ Step 2 · sentence to queryTranslate each rule below into one Gmail search query. Use ONLY these operators: from: to:me cc: subject: - OR { } ( ) category: list: older_than: newer_than: If a rule cannot be expressed with them, say which part and leave it out. Do not invent operators. Rule 1: A person wrote to me directly in the last week, not a promotion or a social update. Rule 2: Anything from my manager (alex@yourcompany.com) or anyone at client-domain.com. Rule 3: Updates and forum mail where I am not in the To line. What you should get back, roughly: 1 to:me -category:promotions -category:social newer_than:7d 2 from:{alex@yourcompany.com client-domain.com} 3 (category:updates OR category:forums) -to:me

Run each query as a search, and count it by hand. Paste it into the Gmail search box. No filter exists yet, so nothing moves. Go through the results from last week and tick each one you actually acted on: replied, forwarded, or did something about. That share is the rule's precision, by hand. Then think the other way: which messages did you act on last week that this search did not return? Those are its misses. For rule 3, the "later" rule, the count you want is the opposite one: how many acted-on messages it would have pushed away.

A rule is earning its place when its precision is clearly above that number.

Only now, create the filter, and give it a label as its only action. From the search box, open the search options and choose "Create filter". Tick "Apply the label" and nothing else. Leave "Skip the Inbox", "Delete it" and "Forward it to" alone. A wrong label costs you one click and nobody else sees it; a wrong delete or forward may never be noticed at all. Menu names were read in October 2026 and may have moved.

★ Step 4 · the filter settingsQuery: to:me -category:promotions -category:social Action: ON Apply the label: triage/first off Skip the Inbox (Archive it) off Delete it off Forward it to Note: newer_than:7d was for counting; drop it from the filter itself.

On Outlook? Outlook has rules too, built on the same idea of conditions on sender, recipients and subject, with actions that range from moving and categorising to deleting and forwarding. They are not walked through here. The method transfers unchanged: write the sentence, find the matching mail with a search first, count it against what you did, and give the first version of the rule a category or a folder, never a delete or a forward.

Keep the counts. Precision and misses for each rule, on a week you labelled yourself, are exactly the kind of number session 4's bench produces on the Enron mail. Bring them, and you will see whether your inbox behaves like Corman's, Keiser's or Heard's.

Homework

Before session 3

20 min, once
  1. Count your base rate. Take last week's inbox and tick every message you acted on. Acted on divided by received is the number every rule and every tool you are offered has to beat.
  2. Finish the hand count for all three rules, results, acted on, precision and misses, and rewrite any query whose precision sits at your base rate.
  3. Let the label filters run for a week and note every message they labelled wrongly, and every one they should have caught. That is next week's evidence, not a reason to switch them off.
  4. Bring the three rules and their counts to session 4's bench. You will write them again as bench rules and see them scored on 390 real messages next to an AI judge.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On a pile of 390 messages where the owners acted on 101, a tool flags every message as important. What is its precision?

Recall is 100 percent, which is the number a flag-everything tool will quote. Precision is hits over flagged, 101 over 390, which is the base rate, lift 1.00. A rule has to climb off that floor to be worth anything.

2 · Where did Gmail Priority Inbox get its "right answer" for which mail is important?

"Importance ground truth is based on how the user interacts with a mail after delivery." Behaviour is the label. It is cheap and honest about actions, and blind to anything done off the email, like a phone call instead of a reply.

3 · Your new rule's search looks right. Why should the first filter you create from it apply a label, rather than delete or forward?

Gmail filters can label, archive, delete, star or forward. Until a rule's precision and misses have been counted against what you really did, give it the one action a mistake cannot hurt. A forwarding filter affects only new messages, which is exactly why it fails silently.
