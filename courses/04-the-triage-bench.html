The triage bench

Sessions 2 and 3 argued that a triage rule is a bet with a base rate under it. Tonight the argument becomes a count. Three hundred and ninety real messages from three Enron mailboxes sit in this page, and each one carries the label its owner gave it by what they did next: replied or forwarded, kept, or deleted. You write rules, or pick them from a ladder, and the page counts how many messages each rule flags, how many of those the owner really acted on, and how many acted-on messages it missed. Then you break it on purpose, to prove it was measuring the rule and not something else.

Part 0

Where we start

A bench is a fixed pile of messages and a fixed set of questions, so that when two rules give two answers the only thing that differed was the rule. The messages are real: 130 drawn with a seeded generator from each of three mailboxes in the public Enron corpus, Shelley Corman's, Kam Keiser's and Marie Heard's. The labels are real too, and they are not anyone's opinion of what mattered. They are what each owner did.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The habit of reading precision next to the base rate before you believe any triage claim, a measured sense of how far header rules can get on a real inbox (about twice the base rate, no further), and a break test you can run on any filter or assistant someone sells you.

Part 1 · covers what the bench measures

A flag is a bet, and the base rate is the house

6 min live

Every rule splits the pile in two: flagged and not flagged. Every message also has a truth: the owner acted on it or did not. Crossing the two gives four counts, and every readout on the bench is a ratio of those four. The one most people skip is the first: how much of the pile was worth acting on before any rule ran.

LiveWhere the labels come from, and why that matters more than the rules3 min▶

Nobody sat down and marked these 390 messages as important. Each label comes from the owner's own mailbox, read after the fact:

This is the same move Gmail's Priority Inbox made in 2010: its engineers wrote that "importance ground truth is based on how the user interacts with a mail after delivery", because asking people to label their mail does not scale and their behaviour is already a label. It is also why the labels are imperfect in known ways. A phone call instead of a reply counts as not acted on. A reply under a new subject line is missed. And these mailboxes were frozen in 2001 and 2002, so some messages labelled read were simply never processed.

Self-studyWhy the bench reports four numbers and not one3 min▶

Flagged share is the cost: how much of your inbox the rule asks you to look at first. Precision is how often a flag is right. Recall is how much of the real work the flag catches. Lift is precision divided by the base rate, which is the only way to compare a rule across two inboxes where different shares of mail need action.

Any one of them alone can be gamed. Recall alone is maximised by flagging everything. Precision alone is maximised by flagging one message you are certain about. The bench always shows all of them because the trade between them is the decision, and Priority Inbox's own engineers said why it cannot be automated away: "users do not agree on the cost of a false positive versus a false negative."

Real world

The three base rates are not the same. In Shelley Corman's 130 messages, 16 were acted on: 12.3 percent. In Kam Keiser's, 40: 30.8 percent. In Marie Heard's, 45: 34.6 percent. The same rule set moves differently in each, and a vendor quoting "90 percent of important mail caught" without saying how much of the mail was important has told you half a fact. Use the mailbox picker on the bench to see it.

Build-along · the bench

Seven rule sets, then yours, computed in front of you

★ 26 min · the main event

Work down the ladder in order. For each rung read the verdict line, then the six readouts, then the misses and false alarms underneath: they are real messages, and reading five of each teaches more than any number. Then build your own in the two rule panels, and finish with the break button.

Start at the anti-lever. "Flag everything urgent" catches every message the owners acted on. Recall 100 percent. Precision 25.9 percent, which is the base rate to the decimal, because a flag on everything is the pile.

Climb to "A person, to me, three or fewer". Not an automated-looking sender, owner in To, at most three recipients. 165 flagged, 42.3 percent of the pile, precision 39.4 percent, recall 64.4 percent. Lift 1.52.

Then "...and a live thread or an ask". The same rule, plus a RE: subject or an ask word. 94 flagged, under a quarter of the pile, at 50.0 percent precision and 46.5 percent recall: lift 1.93. Half the flags are right, and more than half of the real work is now unflagged. That is the trade, measured.

Write your three rules from session 2 into the panels. Every change rescores the 390. Try to beat lift 1.93 without dropping recall under 46.5 percent. Most people cannot, and the misses table shows why: a lot of acted-on mail looks exactly like the mail that was not.

Press the break button. It shuffles the act labels across the 390 messages with a fixed seed. Every rule falls to the base rate within noise, because nothing a rule can see is connected to the shuffled labels any more. If a rule kept its precision after the shuffle, the bench would be measuring something other than the rule.

Read it in this order. Base rate, then flagged share, then precision against the base rate, then recall. A precision figure read without the base rate beside it is the single most common way a triage tool is oversold, and the four-number readout exists so you never do it.

LiveFinding 1: the word "urgent" caught nothing2 min▶

Five of the 390 subjects say urgent, asap or important. The owners acted on none of them. Five is too few to conclude that urgent mail is ignored, and the break button shows why: shuffle the labels and the same five messages score 60 percent by chance, three hits out of five. What the rung does show is that the keyword everyone reaches for first describes almost none of the mail people actually act on. Senders mark urgency; owners act on relationships and threads.

LiveFinding 2: "from my company" is the base rate wearing a badge2 min▶

Internal senders are 72.8 percent of the pile, and 27.8 percent of them were acted on against 25.9 percent overall. Lift 1.07. The rule feels sensible because most of the mail you act on is internal, and that is exactly the trap: most of the mail you do not act on is internal too. A condition that is true of nearly everything carries nearly no information. The break button barely moves it, because there was almost nothing to break.

LiveFinding 3: who it is addressed to beats what it says2 min▶

The two conditions that move precision are both about the envelope: is the owner in To rather than Cc, and is the recipient list short. Adding "not an automated-looking sender" takes the rule from 34.5 to 39.4 percent. The content conditions, a RE: subject or an ask word, add the last ten points but cost eighteen points of recall. This matches what Priority Inbox's engineers listed first among their features: "Social features are based on the degree of interaction between sender and recipient."

A caution that belongs next to the RE: condition: part of its edge comes from how the label is built. A thread the owner is already in has more of the owner's replies with that subject to match, so "RE:" partly predicts the label by construction. The bench says so rather than claiming the whole ten points.

LiveFinding 4: the AI judge, replayed3 min▶

No model runs in this page. Once, offline, on 5 October 2026, a real model read each of the 390 messages and answered act, read or archive: qwen2.5-coder:7b, a small open model run locally through Ollama at temperature 0, seeing exactly what the rules see, the envelope plus the first 160 characters of the body. Its answers ship as data and the bench replays them. It is the model that was available on the build machine, not a frontier model, and the page claims a measurement of that one run and nothing wider.

It flagged 152 messages, 39.0 percent of the pile, at 34.9 percent precision and 52.5 percent recall: lift 1.35, about where "addressed to me" sits and below the two best header rules. Across all three labels it agreed with what the owners did on 46.4 percent of messages, and it called only 25 messages archive where the owners deleted 111 without acting. Under the break button its precision falls to 26.3 percent, so it did learn something real from the text. On Marie Heard's mailbox alone it reaches 43.7 percent precision at 68.9 percent recall, and on Kam Keiser's it scores below that mailbox's base rate. The lesson is not that models lose to rules. It is that a judge's claim gets the same four numbers and the same break test as any rule, and this one, on this mail, did not earn its place over a three-condition filter.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the break

Flag everything, then shuffle everything

4 min live

Two moves make a bench honest. The anti-lever shows the number a lazy rule gets, so nobody mistakes 100 percent recall for a result. The break shows the number any rule gets when the labels mean nothing, so nobody mistakes noise for skill.

LiveWhy a bench needs a number it can lose2 min▶

If no input could make the bench report a worse number, it would not be measuring anything. The anti-lever proves the bottom exists: precision equal to the base rate, lift 1.00. The break proves the rules earned their lift from the labels and not from a quirk of the data: with the act labels shuffled, the person rung falls from 39.4 to 27.3 percent and the thread rung from 50.0 to 23.4 percent, both inside the noise around 25.9.

Run the same test on anything that claims to triage for you. Feed it a week of your mail where you know what you acted on, compare its flags to that, and ask what its precision would be if the flags were random. If the vendor cannot answer the second question, the first number is a press release.

Self-studyWhat "flag everything urgent" looks like in a real office2 min▶

It rarely looks like a rule. It looks like a culture where every message carries the red exclamation mark, every request says asap, and every inbox notification is on. Recall is perfect, nothing is ever missed, and every message gets the same attention, which means the 25.9 percent that needed action got no more of it than the 74.1 percent that did not. The bench gives it a number so the conversation can stop being about effort.

Real world

Gmail measured its own version of this. Priority Inbox reported about 80 percent accuracy, plus or minus 5, on its implicit importance label, and its engineers tuned thresholds so that false negatives ran three to four times false positives, because many people read mail they agree is not important. On 160,000 messages users marked by hand, error fell from 45 percent with one global model to 31 percent with per-user models and per-user thresholds. Personal ops is personal: the same rule cannot be right for Corman at a 12.3 percent base rate and Heard at 34.6.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Label one real week of your own mail by what you did: replied or forwarded, kept, deleted. Count the act share. That is your base rate, and it is the number every tool you are sold should be compared against.
  2. Run your best bench rule as a mail search over that week (session 2 has the operators). Count flagged, hits and misses by hand. Your lift is precision divided by your base rate.
  3. Read five of your own misses. Write one line on why each was acted on. Most people find the reason is not in the message at all: who sent it, what was said in a meeting, what happened yesterday.
  4. Bring one assistant or tool that touches your mail or calendar. Session 5 is about what you let it do.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · "Flag everything urgent" catches 100 percent of the messages the owners acted on. What is its precision on the bench, and why?

Recall and precision trade against each other. Flagging all 390 gives recall 100 percent and precision 101 out of 390, the share of mail that was acted on before any rule ran. Lift 1.00 is the floor every real rule must beat.

2 · After the labels are shuffled, the five-message "urgent" rule shows 60 percent precision. What should you conclude?

With five flagged messages, one more or one fewer hit moves precision by 20 points. The rules that flag 94 and 165 messages fall to 23.4 and 27.3 percent under the shuffle, inside the noise around the 25.9 percent base rate. Small counts lie loudly in both directions.

3 · The best header rule reaches 50.0 percent precision at 46.5 percent recall. What is the honest summary?

50 percent against a 25.9 percent base rate is lift 1.93, which is real and useful for ordering what you read first. It is not a reason to hide the other 53.5 percent of acted-on mail, and one bench on three 2001 mailboxes proves nothing about every judge.
